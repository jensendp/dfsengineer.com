A "value play" is a player projected to outproduce their salary relative
to the rest of the slate — not just a cheap player, and not just a good
player, but a specific ratio between the two. Chasing pure salary
efficiency without context is how you end up rostering a punt play with
no real path to a useful score. This page explains the concept once;
each week's post below applies it to that week's real slate.

## The math, plainly

The number most DFS tools show is **value per $1,000 of salary**:
`projected_points / (salary / 1000)`. A $5,000 receiver projected for 12
points scores a 2.4. A $9,000 receiver projected for 18 points also
scores a 2.4 — same efficiency, very different role on your roster.

Value on its own doesn't tell you:

- **Floor vs. ceiling.** A cheap player with a high floor is a cash-game
  value. A cheap player with a wide range of outcomes (touchdown-or-bust)
  is a GPP value. The same value score can describe either.
- **Salary relief vs. genuine alpha.** Sometimes the highest-value player
  on the board is there because they're the *best* real play at their
  price, and sometimes they're there because the field is priced up
  around a name and the algorithm hasn't caught a real role change yet.
  Both show up as "high value" — only one is worth building around.
- **Matchup context.** A cheap player's value spikes when the matchup
  data supports it (see the [matchup analysis hub](/blog/nfl-dfs-matchup-analysis/))
  — a favorable matchup is often *why* the projection is high enough to
  clear the value bar in the first place.

## How to use a value board without overreacting to it

1. Start from the top of the value list, but check *why* each player is
   there before locking them in — role, matchup, injury status upstream
   of them (a backup only has value if the starter's actually out).
2. Weigh value differently by contest type. Cash games want value with a
   real floor; GPPs can afford to chase a volatile, high-ceiling value
   play the field is under-rostering.
3. Treat salary relief as a tool, not a strategy. The point of finding
   value at one or two spots is to afford a stud elsewhere on the
   roster — not to fill every slot with the cheapest efficient option and
   end up with a lineup that has no real ceiling.
