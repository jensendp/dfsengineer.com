## What Is NFL DFS and How Does It Actually Work {#what-is-nfl-dfs}

Daily fantasy sports took the concept of season-long fantasy football and compressed it into a single week. Instead of drafting a team in August and managing it through January, you build a brand-new roster every Sunday. Your players score points based on their real NFL performance that day, and you compete against other lineups for cash prizes.

The "daily" part is what makes it different from the fantasy league you might already play with friends or coworkers. There's no waiver wire. No trade negotiations. No nursing a injured quarterback for six weeks hoping he comes back. Every slate is a fresh start.

Here's the basic loop:

1. **Choose a contest.** You pick a tournament, head-to-head matchup, or other contest type on a platform like DraftKings or FanDuel.
2. **Build a roster.** You select NFL players under a salary cap. Each player has a price, and you can't exceed the total budget.
3. **Watch the games.** Your players accumulate fantasy points based on their real-world stats — touchdowns, yards, receptions, and so on.
4. **Get paid (or don't).** If your lineup scores enough points relative to the competition, you win a share of the prize pool.

That's the skeleton. The muscle and connective tissue — salary cap strategy, contest selection, game theory, correlation — is where the real skill lives, and where this guide spends most of its time.

### The Two Major Platforms

**DraftKings** and **FanDuel** dominate the NFL DFS market. They operate on the same basic principle but differ in ways that matter once you start building lineups:

| Feature | DraftKings | FanDuel |
|---|---|---|
| Salary cap | $50,000 | $60,000 |
| Roster size | 9 players (QB, RB, RB, WR, WR, WR, TE, FLEX, DST) | 9 players (QB, RB, RB, WR, WR, WR, TE, FLEX, DST) |
| PPR scoring | Full PPR (1 point per reception) | Half PPR (0.5 points per reception) |
| Bonus points | Yes (300-yard passing bonuses, 100-yard rushing/receiving bonuses) | No milestone bonuses |

These differences shape strategy in meaningful ways. The full-point PPR on DraftKings makes pass-catching running backs and high-volume receivers more valuable. The bonus thresholds on DraftKings reward players who hit specific yardage milestones, which slightly changes how you evaluate ceiling. FanDuel's half-PPR format tilts value back toward pure yardage and touchdowns.

Most serious DFS players eventually play on both platforms. For your first few weeks, pick one and learn its quirks before splitting attention.

### Is It Legal? Is It Gambling?

DFS operates in a legal gray area that has mostly resolved itself through state-by-state regulation. The federal government carved out fantasy sports from the Unlawful Internet Gambling Enforcement Act of 2006, classifying them as games of skill rather than chance. Most states have since passed specific legislation allowing DFS, though a handful have restricted or banned it.

The "skill vs. chance" debate is real but largely settled in practice. Over a large enough sample, skilled players win consistently and unskilled players lose consistently — a pattern you wouldn't see in pure gambling. That said, any single week involves enormous variance. A backup running back can score three touchdowns because the starter pulled a hamstring during warmups. Skill shows up over hundreds or thousands of contests, not in any individual one.

Check your state's current laws before depositing money. DraftKings and FanDuel both block access from states where DFS isn't permitted.

## The Core Mechanics: Salary Cap, Scoring, and Roster Construction {#core-mechanics}

Understanding the mechanical rules of DFS is like understanding the rules of poker before you sit at a table. You need to know what you're working with before strategy makes any sense.

### The Salary Cap

Every player on a DFS slate has a salary — a dollar amount set by the platform that reflects how much production they're expected to deliver. Patrick Mahomes on a good matchup week might cost $8,200 on DraftKings. A backup tight end on a bad team might cost $2,500.

Your job is to fill every roster slot without exceeding the total cap. On DraftKings, that's $50,000 across nine players. On FanDuel, it's $60,000 across nine players.

This constraint is the entire game. If you could just pick the nine best players every week, there would be no skill involved. The cap forces tradeoffs. Spending up for an elite quarterback means you need cheaper options at other positions. Finding an underpriced player who outperforms his salary — "value" — is what creates the edge.

**A salary isn't a prediction.** It's a market price. Salaries are set by the platform's algorithms early in the week and don't change (unlike betting lines, which move). This means the platform can underprice players who see late-breaking positive news, like a teammate's injury opening up more targets. Those pricing inefficiencies are where a lot of profit hides.

### Scoring Systems

Here's how points accumulate on each platform:

**DraftKings Scoring:**

| Action | Points |
|---|---|
| Passing TD | +4 |
| Passing yard | +0.04 (1 point per 25 yards) |
| Passing interception | -1 |
| Rushing/Receiving TD | +6 |
| Rushing/Receiving yard | +0.1 (1 point per 10 yards) |
| Reception | +1 (Full PPR) |
| Fumble lost | -1 |
| 2-point conversion | +2 |
| 300+ passing yards bonus | +3 |
| 100+ rushing yards bonus | +3 |
| 100+ receiving yards bonus | +3 |

**FanDuel Scoring:**

| Action | Points |
|---|---|
| Passing TD | +4 |
| Passing yard | +0.04 (1 point per 25 yards) |
| Passing interception | -1 |
| Rushing/Receiving TD | +6 |
| Rushing/Receiving yard | +0.1 (1 point per 10 yards) |
| Reception | +0.5 (Half PPR) |
| Fumble lost | -2 |
| 2-point conversion | +2 |

The key takeaways from these tables:

- **Touchdowns are king.** A single rushing or receiving touchdown is worth 6 points — the equivalent of 60 rushing yards or 150 passing yards. This is why touchdown-dependent players are volatile but valuable.
- **Volume matters in PPR formats.** On DraftKings, a running back who catches 6 passes for 30 yards gets 9 points (6 reception points + 3 yard points) before any rushing stats. That same line on FanDuel is only 6 points. This changes who you target.
- **Quarterbacks are efficient point producers.** A QB who throws for 280 yards and 2 TDs with no picks scores 19.2 points on both platforms. That's a pedestrian game. Elite QB performances regularly hit 30+ points.
- **Interceptions and fumbles hurt, but not catastrophically.** A -1 for an interception stings, but it doesn't wipe out a good game. Don't avoid high-upside QBs just because they occasionally throw picks.

### Roster Construction Basics

Both platforms use the same general roster structure:

- **QB** (1): Your quarterback
- **RB** (2): Two running backs
- **WR** (3): Three wide receivers
- **TE** (1): One tight end
- **FLEX** (1): Any RB, WR, or TE
- **DST** (1): Team defense/special teams

The FLEX spot is where most of the strategic flexibility lives. It's an extra slot that can be any skill position player, and deciding whether to put a third RB, fourth WR, or second TE there shapes your entire roster construction.

**Common roster builds by position allocation (RB/WR/TE in the FLEX):**

- **3 RB / 3 WR / 1 TE:** The "balanced" build. You're betting that running back volume and touchdowns will carry you alongside your receivers.
- **2 RB / 4 WR / 1 TE:** The most common GPP build on DraftKings. Wide receivers have higher ceilings in full PPR, and getting a fourth one gives you more paths to a big score.
- **2 RB / 3 WR / 2 TE:** The "double tight end" build. Rare, but viable when a second tight end is priced cheaply and has a strong matchup. Travis Kelce at TE1 with a $3,000 punt at TE2 can work if that cheap option hits.

Don't overthink this early on. The 2 RB / 4 WR / 1 TE build is a fine default for tournaments on DraftKings. On FanDuel, where receptions are worth less, the 3 RB / 3 WR / 1 TE build holds more value because running backs retain more of their scoring floor through rushing volume.

## Contest Types Explained: Cash Games vs. GPP Tournaments {#contest-types}

Not all DFS contests are created equal, and the strategy for winning them diverges sharply. This is one of the first things beginners need to internalize: **the lineup that wins a tournament is usually a terrible cash game lineup, and vice versa.**

### Cash Games

Cash games are contests where roughly 50% of entries win and 50% lose. They include:

- **Head-to-Head (H2H):** You vs. one other person. Higher score wins and roughly doubles your entry fee (minus the platform's cut, called "rake" or "vig").
- **50/50s:** A pool of players where the top half doubles their money and the bottom half gets nothing.
- **Double-Ups:** Similar to 50/50s but sometimes with a slightly different payout structure (e.g., top 44% get 1.8x their entry).

**Cash game strategy is about floor, not ceiling.** You need to beat roughly half the field, which means you need a lineup that's reliably good rather than explosively great. This means:

- Prioritizing players with high snap counts, targets, and carries — the volume metrics that create consistent production
- Avoiding risky, low-owned players who might score 2 points or 25 points
- Spending up for safe, expensive studs rather than hunting for cheap lottery tickets
- Not worrying about being "unique" — if 60% of the field is playing the same quarterback, and that quarterback is the clear best play, you should too

**The mindset:** Don't lose, rather than try to win big.

### GPP Tournaments (Guaranteed Prize Pools)

GPPs are the marquee contests of DFS — large-field tournaments where thousands or hundreds of thousands of entries compete for a top-heavy prize pool. First place might pay $100,000 while 25th place pays $500.

**Tournament strategy is about ceiling, differentiation, and leverage.** You need a lineup that can outscore tens of thousands of other lineups, which requires:

- **High-upside players.** You want players who can realistically score 30+ fantasy points, even if they also have a chance of scoring 5. Ceiling over floor.
- **Contrarian thinking.** If everyone in the tournament rosters the same quarterback, even if he has a great game, he doesn't help you gain ground on the field. Finding underowned players who pop off is how you separate.
- **Correlated stacks.** Instead of nine independent players, you want groups of teammates whose success is linked. More on this in the correlation chapter.
- **Willingness to be wrong.** Your tournament lineup will fail far more often than it succeeds. That's the point. You're swinging for a low-probability, high-payoff outcome.

### A Concrete Example of the Strategic Difference

Suppose it's Week 8 and Josh Allen is the highest-projected quarterback on the slate. He costs $8,400 on DraftKings and is projected for 24.5 points. He'll be 28% owned in both cash games and tournaments.

**In a cash game:** You play Josh Allen. He's the best projection, his floor is high because of his rushing upside, and being "chalky" (highly owned) doesn't matter because you just need to beat half the field.

**In a tournament:** You might *skip* Josh Allen specifically because he's 28% owned. If he scores 25 points, he doesn't separate you from the 28% of lineups that also have him. Instead, you might play Jalen Hurts at $7,600 and 12% ownership. If Hurts outscores Allen, you've gained leverage on a huge chunk of the field. If Hurts underperforms, your tournament entry was probably dead anyway — and that's acceptable because you only need to win one out of many entries.

This is the fundamental tension of DFS strategy, and it runs through every decision you make.

### Other Contest Types

- **Single-Entry:** Only one lineup allowed per person. This limits the advantage that high-volume players have and is often recommended for beginners.
- **3-Max / 20-Max / 150-Max:** Limits on how many entries one person can submit. Lower max-entry contests are generally softer fields.
- **Showdown/Captain Mode:** A single-game format where you pick players from just one NFL game. One player is designated "Captain" and scores 1.5x points but costs 1.5x salary. These contests require specific strategies beyond the scope of a beginner guide, but they're fun for primetime games.

**Beginner recommendation:** Start with single-entry cash games (50/50s or H2Hs) to learn lineup construction, then move to single-entry GPPs once you understand ownership dynamics and correlation.

## Bankroll Management: The Math That Keeps You in the Game {#bankroll-management}

Here's the uncomfortable truth about DFS: even skilled players have losing weeks. Lots of them. Variance in NFL DFS is massive — a single game can blow up your lineup through injuries, weather, or a defensive coordinator deciding to double-team your star receiver all day.

Bankroll management is the discipline that prevents those inevitable losing weeks from ending your DFS career.

### What Is a Bankroll?

Your bankroll is the total amount of money you've set aside specifically for DFS. It's not your rent money. It's not your savings. It's money you can afford to lose entirely without it affecting your life. Setting this boundary before you deposit a single dollar is the most important financial decision you'll make in DFS.

### The Rules

**For cash games:** Risk no more than **5% of your bankroll** on any single slate.

If your bankroll is $200, you should be entering no more than $10 worth of cash games on a given Sunday. That might be two $5 head-to-heads or ten $1 double-ups.

**For tournaments:** Risk no more than **10-15% of your bankroll** on a given slate, divided across multiple entries if you're playing multi-entry contests.

If your bankroll is $200, you might spend $20-$30 on tournament entries for a Sunday main slate. That could be three $10 single-entry GPPs, or thirty $1 entries in a large-field tournament.

**Why these numbers?** They come from a concept borrowed from sports betting and poker called the Kelly Criterion — a mathematical framework for sizing bets to maximize long-term growth while minimizing risk of ruin (going broke). The exact optimal percentages depend on your edge, which you won't know as a beginner. These conservative guidelines keep you solvent while you learn.

### The Trap: Chasing Losses

You deposit $100. You lose $30 in Week 1. You think: "I need to bet bigger in Week 2 to make it back." So you enter $40 worth of contests. You lose again. Now you're down $70 and the desperation math gets worse.

This is the fastest way to bust your bankroll. The correct response to a losing week is to stick to the same percentage-based approach. If your bankroll is now $70 after losing $30, your cash game budget for next week is $3.50, not $40.

**It feels wrong. It's mathematically right.** Your bankroll will recover if you have skill. It just takes patience and discipline.

### Record-Keeping

Track every contest entry, every result, every dollar. A simple spreadsheet works:

| Date | Platform | Contest Type | Entry Fee | Winnings | Net | Running Bankroll |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | DraftKings | $5 H2H | $5.00 | $9.00 | +$4.00 | $204.00 |
| Week 1 | DraftKings | $3 GPP | $3.00 | $0.00 | -$3.00 | $201.00 |
| Week 1 | FanDuel | $1 50/50 (x5) | $5.00 | $9.00 | +$4.00 | $205.00 |

This data becomes invaluable over time. You'll see which contest types you're profitable in, which platform suits your style, and whether your skill is actually translating into results or if you're just running hot.

## The Data-Driven Lineup Building Process {#lineup-building-process}

Building a DFS lineup isn't about picking your favorite players or going with your gut on who'll "have a big game." It's a systematic process that starts with data and ends with a roster that gives you the best chance of winning the specific contest type you've entered.

Here's the process from start to finish.

### Step 1: Identify the Slate and Lock Time

NFL DFS offers several slate types each week:

- **Main Slate:** The Sunday 1:00 PM ET games (typically 8-10 games). This is the most popular and where most strategy content focuses.
- **Afternoon Slate:** The Sunday 4:00 PM ET games (typically 3-4 games).
- **Sunday Night / Monday Night / Thursday Night:** Single-game or small slates.
- **Full Sunday / Full Weekend:** All games combined.

Start with the **main slate** for Sunday early games. It has the largest player pool, the most contest variety, and the most available analysis. Smaller slates reduce the player pool and increase variance, making them harder to navigate for beginners.

### Step 2: Study the Vegas Lines

Before looking at any player, look at the game-level data. Sportsbook lines are among the most predictive tools available because they represent the collective wisdom of sharp money. Focus on two numbers:

**Over/Under (Total):** The expected combined score of both teams. Games with high totals (48+) project to be high-scoring, which means more fantasy points available for players in those games. Low totals (under 40) suggest defensive slugfests where fantasy production will be scarce.

**Spread:** The expected margin of victory. A team favored by 7+ points is expected to build a lead and potentially run the ball in the second half, which is good for their running backs and bad for their passing game volume (since they won't need to throw). Conversely, a team that's a 7+ point underdog may need to throw frequently in the second half to catch up, boosting their passing game.

**The sweet spot for fantasy production:** Games with high totals and close spreads. A 48-total game with a 2.5-point spread projects a competitive, high-scoring game — exactly the environment where quarterbacks, receivers, and everyone else put up numbers.

### Step 3: Evaluate Matchups and Defensive Metrics

Not all defenses are created equal, and the gap between the best and worst pass defenses (or rush defenses) is significant for fantasy purposes.

Key metrics to check:

- **DVOA (Defense-adjusted Value Over Average):** Football Outsiders' metric that measures defensive efficiency adjusted for opponent and situation. A defense ranked 30th in pass DVOA is getting shredded through the air.
- **Fantasy Points Allowed by Position:** Many sites compile how many fantasy points each defense has allowed to each position. If the Cowboys have allowed the 3rd-most fantasy points to wide receivers, that's useful context for rostering a WR playing Dallas.
- **Adjusted sack rate and pressure rate:** Defenses that generate heavy pressure make quarterbacks less reliable and more turnover-prone. This matters when evaluating QB matchups.

**A warning about defensive metrics:** Small sample sizes in the NFL (17-game seasons, and you're often evaluating just 4-6 weeks of data) make these numbers noisy. A defense might rank 32nd against the run because they played three elite rushing offenses in their first four games, not because they're actually bad. Context matters more than raw rankings.

### Step 4: Identify Value Players and Salary Savers

Your lineup needs both high-priced studs and bargain-bin value plays to fit under the cap. Finding underpriced players is the single most important skill in DFS.

**What creates value?**

- **Injury to a teammate:** If a starting running back is ruled out, his backup's salary probably hasn't adjusted to reflect the increased workload. A $4,500 backup getting 20 carries is tremendous value.
- **Recent role change:** A wide receiver who's been averaging 4 targets per game but just saw 11 targets last week because another WR got hurt. His salary might not have caught up.
- **Matchup-driven upside:** A mediocre tight end facing a defense that's been hemorrhaging points to tight ends. He's $3,200 because he's mediocre overall, but this specific week he has a path to 15 points.
- **Ownership driving price down:** Sometimes a good player on a bad team gets discounted because the team is expected to lose. But losing teams throw the ball, and garbage-time stats count the same in fantasy.

### Step 5: Build the Core of Your Lineup

Start with your strongest convictions — the 3-4 players you feel best about based on matchup, price, and projected usage. These are your "core" plays.

For a tournament lineup, this core typically includes:

- A quarterback you want to build a stack around (more on stacking in the next chapter)
- 1-2 high-priced skill players with elite matchups
- 1-2 value plays that free up salary for your studs

For a cash game lineup, the core is:

- The safest high-volume players at each position
- Running backs with heavy workloads and passing-game involvement
- Wide receivers with high target shares on good offenses

### Step 6: Fill Remaining Slots and Optimize

Once your core is set, fill the remaining roster spots while staying under the salary cap. This is where you'll make tradeoffs:

- "I love this WR at $6,800 but I can't afford him with my current core. Can I downgrade my TE from $5,200 to $3,500 and still feel good about it?"
- "I have $4,100 left for my DST. Which defense in that price range has the best matchup?"

Many players use **optimizer tools** for this step — software that takes your player projections and constraints and spits out mathematically optimal lineups. These tools are useful but not magical. The quality of the output depends entirely on the quality of the projections you feed in. Garbage in, garbage out.

If you're building lineups by hand, just cycle through combinations methodically. There's no shame in spending 45 minutes swapping players around on a Saturday night.

## Ownership and Game Theory: The Layer Most Beginners Miss {#ownership-and-game-theory}

Here's where DFS gets intellectually interesting and where intermediate strategy separates from beginner strategy.

### What Is Ownership?

Ownership is the percentage of lineups in a contest that roster a given player. If Josh Allen is "25% owned" in a tournament, that means 25% of all entries have him at quarterback.

Platforms don't show you exact ownership numbers before lock — you'd just copy the lowest-owned optimal lineup if they did. But several factors make ownership **highly predictable:**

- Players on the DraftKings and FanDuel "featured" or popular pages get over-rostered
- Players highlighted by major DFS media (podcasts, YouTube shows, articles) get over-rostered
- Players with obvious narratives ("revenge game," "best matchup on the slate") get over-rostered
- Players who performed well last week get over-rostered (recency bias)

Various DFS tools and sites publish **projected ownership** estimates that are reasonably accurate. These projections are based on historical patterns, slate analysis, and market signals.

### Why Ownership Matters in Tournaments

Imagine a 10-person tournament where first place wins everything. You and three other people all roster the same quarterback. He scores 30 points — a great game. But you haven't gained any ground on those three people. Your QB effectively canceled out. You need the *rest* of your lineup to beat them, and you need to beat the other six entrants who took different QBs.

Now imagine you're the only person in the tournament who rostered a different quarterback, and he also scores 30 points. You just gained ground on all nine opponents at the most impactful position.

**This is leverage.** In tournaments, you get paid for being *different and right.* Being the same and right just keeps you even.

The math works like this:

- **High-owned player booms:** Everyone who has him benefits. You don't gain an edge.
- **High-owned player busts:** Everyone who has him suffers. You gain an edge if you faded him.
- **Low-owned player booms:** Only you (and the few others who have him) benefit. Massive edge.
- **Low-owned player busts:** Only you suffer, but you were a long shot anyway.

In tournaments, the asymmetry of low-owned hits vs. high-owned hits favors contrarian plays — *as long as the contrarian play has a legitimate path to a big game.* Being contrarian for its own sake, rostering bad players just because nobody else has them, is a great way to finish last.

### Why Ownership Doesn't Matter in Cash Games

In a 50/50, you just need to finish in the top half. There's no extra reward for finishing first vs. finishing 25th percentile. This means leverage is irrelevant — you don't need to differentiate from the field, you just need to beat half of it. Play the best players regardless of ownership.

### The Ownership Sweet Spot for Tournaments

You're looking for players who are:

- **Underowned relative to their projection.** If a player projects as the 3rd-best WR on the slate but is only the 12th-most owned, that's a leverage opportunity.
- **Underowned because of a narratively unappealing situation.** Playing in London, coming off a bad game, "boring" player on a "boring" team. The betting market doesn't care about narratives; the DFS market does.
- **Low-owned with correlated upside.** A cheap receiver on a team in a game with a high total. He might be 4% owned, and if his team's offense erupts, he could catch a couple touchdowns. That's the dream in GPPs.

### Game Theory in Practice

Game theory in DFS is a deep rabbit hole, but the beginner version is straightforward:

1. **Estimate what the field will do.** Which players will be highly owned? Which game stacks will be popular?
2. **Decide which popular plays you agree with and which you want to fade.** Don't fade a play just because it's popular. Fade it because you think the field is overweighting it relative to alternatives.
3. **Find spots where the field is underweighting quality plays.** These are your leverage opportunities.
4. **Accept the variance.** Game theory-optimal lineups lose more often than "chalk" lineups. They also win bigger when they hit. That's the deal.

## Correlation and Stacking: Why Lineup Structure Matters More Than Player Picks {#correlation-and-stacking}

This chapter might be the most important one for tournament players. The concept is simple but its implications are profound: **your players' outcomes are not independent of each other, and you should exploit that.**

### What Is Correlation?

Correlation in DFS means that when one player in your lineup does well, another player in your lineup is also likely to do well — because their outcomes are linked.

The most obvious example: a quarterback and his wide receiver. If the QB throws for 350 yards and 4 touchdowns, his top WR probably caught several of those passes and scored at least one of those touchdowns. Their fantasy performances are positively correlated.

A less obvious example: a running back and the opposing quarterback. If the opposing QB is lighting up a defense, the running back's team is probably losing and abandoning the run game. Their performances are *negatively* correlated.

### Why Stacking Works

A **stack** is a group of correlated players in your lineup, typically a QB paired with one or more of his pass catchers.

Here's why stacking is mathematically superior to picking nine unrelated players in tournaments:

Without stacking, you need nine *independent* good performances to have a great lineup. The probability of all nine hitting is low because you're diversified across many different game scripts and outcomes.

With stacking, you need one *game environment* to break right, and multiple players in your lineup benefit simultaneously. If the Chiefs-Bills game turns into a 38-35 shootout, your Allen-Diggs-Knox stack might collectively score 80 points — and those 80 points are correlated, meaning they all happened because of the same event (a high-scoring game).

**Stacking increases your lineup's ceiling at the cost of its floor.** If that Chiefs-Bills game is a 13-10 snooze fest instead, your entire stack disappoints simultaneously. That's fine in a tournament where you need a ceiling outcome anyway.

### Common Stack Structures

**QB + WR1 (Mini Stack):**
The simplest stack. You pair a quarterback with his top wide receiver. When the QB throws a touchdown, there's a good chance it's to his WR1.

**QB + WR1 + WR2 or TE (Full Stack):**
A three-player stack from the same team's passing game. This concentrates your lineup even more heavily into one game environment. Higher ceiling, lower floor.

**QB + WR1 + Opposing WR (Game Stack or "Bring-Back"):**
This is the gold standard for tournament stacking. You take a QB and his receiver, then also roster a pass catcher from the opposing team. Why? Because if your QB is throwing for 350 yards, the game is probably high-scoring. And if the game is high-scoring, the opposing team's passing game is also producing. The "bring-back" player captures the other side of a shootout.

**Example:** Josh Allen + Stefon Diggs + Tyreek Hill (when they were on opposing teams). If Allen is dealing, the Bills are probably scoring, which means the Chiefs are probably in a pass-heavy game script too, which means Hill is getting targets.

**RB + DST (Negative Correlation — Avoid in Stacks):**
If your running back is on a team that's winning big, the opposing defense is getting blown out. Don't pair a running back with the defense playing against his team.

### Stacking Rules of Thumb for Beginners

1. **Always stack your QB with at least one pass catcher.** A "naked" QB (no stack) in a tournament is a strategic mistake. You're leaving correlated upside on the table.

2. **Use a bring-back in tournaments.** Your QB stack + one opposing pass catcher covers both sides of a high-scoring game.

3. **Don't stack more than 4 players from the same game.** You start hitting diminishing returns and over-concentrating. Three (QB + pass catcher + bring-back) is the sweet spot.

4. **Secondary stacks are powerful.** Beyond your primary QB stack, consider pairing two players from another game — like a RB and a WR from opposite teams, or two WRs from opposite teams in a projected shootout.

5. **In cash games, stacking is optional but still useful.** A simple QB + WR1 stack is fine in cash games. You don't need the bring-back or elaborate multi-game correlations.

### Negative Correlation: What to Avoid

Some pairings work against each other:

- **RB + Opposing RB from the same game:** If one team is running the ball successfully, they're probably winning, which means the other team is probably passing. Both RBs being dominant in the same game is unlikely.
- **QB + Opposing DST:** If your QB is scoring fantasy points, the opposing defense is giving up real points. Direct negative correlation.
- **DST + High-scoring game players:** A defense that scores well in fantasy usually means the game was low-scoring, which means skill players in that game underperformed.

## Projections, Rankings, and How to Actually Use Data {#projections-and-data}

The DFS ecosystem is overflowing with data,
