## What NFL DFS Actually Is — And How It Differs from Season-Long Fantasy

Daily fantasy football is a single-week contest. You draft a roster under a fixed salary cap — $50,000 on DraftKings, $60,000 on FanDuel — and your players score points based on their real NFL stats that week. Finish high enough in the standings, you win money. The slate resets, and you start fresh next week. If you're brand new and want a plain-English explanation of the format before diving into strategy, the [NFL DFS Glossary](https://dfsengineer.com/blog/nfl-dfs-glossary) covers every term you'll encounter on the platforms.

That "start fresh" part is the fundamental break from season-long fantasy. There are no trades, no waiver wire claims, no roster spots wasted on a running back who got hurt in Week 3. Every decision exists in isolation. You drafted a bust last Sunday? Irrelevant. There's no sunk cost dragging your roster down for 14 more weeks.

The platforms matter more than beginners expect. DraftKings rosters carry six skill positions plus a FLEX spot, use full-PPR scoring, and award 6 points per passing touchdown. FanDuel uses nine roster slots, half-PPR, and 4-point passing touchdowns. That gap — 6-point vs. 4-point passing TDs — directly changes how much salary you should spend on quarterbacks. A three-touchdown game on DraftKings generates 6 extra points over FanDuel, which is why QB stacking hits harder there.

The next section walks through those differences with exact point totals so you can see the math yourself.

## Scoring Systems Broken Down: DraftKings vs. FanDuel With Real Point Examples

The scoring difference between platforms isn't trivial. It changes who you draft and how you build.

Here's the same QB performance scored on both sites — 280 passing yards, 2 passing TDs, 1 INT:

**DraftKings:** 280 × 0.04 (11.2) + 2 × 6 (12) − 1 × 1 (−1) = **22.2 points**

**FanDuel:** 280 × 0.04 (11.2) + 2 × 4 (8) − 1 × 1 (−1) = **18.2 points**

Four points from one stat line. Scale it up to a 3-TD game and the DraftKings score pulls further ahead. This is why QB is the premier position on DraftKings — the ceiling is structurally higher, which makes QB the natural anchor for lineup construction. On FanDuel, QBs score closer to other positions, so paying up at QB carries less inherent edge.

The table below shows how the same stat lines play across both platforms at key positions so you can see the gaps at a glance:

| Position | Stat Line | DraftKings Score | FanDuel Score | Difference |
|----------|-----------|-----------------|---------------|------------|
| QB | 280 yds, 2 TD, 1 INT | 22.2 | 18.2 | +4.0 DK |
| QB | 320 yds, 3 TD, 0 INT | 30.8 | 24.8 | +6.0 DK |
| RB | 85 rush yds, 1 TD, 5 rec, 30 rec yds | 22.5 | 20.0 | +2.5 DK |
| WR | 6 rec, 95 yds, 1 TD | 21.5 | 18.5 | +3.0 DK |
| TE | 4 rec, 55 yds, 0 TD | 9.5 | 7.5 | +2.0 DK |

Reception scoring widens the platform gap at other positions. A running back who catches 5 passes for 30 yards gets 5 full PPR points on DraftKings but only 2.5 on FanDuel. That difference matters when you're evaluating pass-catching backs or slot receivers who rack up 7–8 receptions on high volume. On DraftKings, those reception-floor players become legitimate cash-game anchors. On FanDuel, their advantage shrinks enough that you weight rushing volume and touchdown equity more heavily.

> **Tip:** Treat DraftKings and FanDuel as two different games, because they are. Build for each platform separately — never copy a DraftKings lineup into FanDuel and expect similar results.

## The Salary Cap: How to Think About $50,000 in Real Terms

Every lineup on DraftKings starts with $50,000. That number means nothing until you translate it into points.

The core metric: projected points per $1,000 of salary. Take a $4,800 RB projected for 12 points — that's 2.5 points per $1,000. Now compare him to a $7,200 RB projected for 16 points — that's 2.22 points per $1,000. The cheaper back delivers more production per dollar spent, and the $2,400 you save can upgrade another roster spot. This is what "value" actually means. Not a gut feeling. A ratio you can calculate for every player in the pool.

Here's the actual calculation you'd run in a spreadsheet:

```
Value Score = Projected Points ÷ (Salary ÷ 1000)

Example A: RB at $4,800, projected 12.0 pts
  Value = 12.0 ÷ 4.8 = 2.50 pts per $1K

Example B: RB at $7,200, projected 16.0 pts
  Value = 16.0 ÷ 7.2 = 2.22 pts per $1K

Savings from picking A over B: $2,400
Points sacrificed: 4.0
Cost per point sacrificed: $2,400 ÷ 4.0 = $600 per point

If you can redeploy that $2,400 at a position where
$600 buys MORE than 1 point, you come out ahead.
```

Winning cash lineups on DraftKings typically spend heavy at QB and one RB slot ($7,000+), moderate at WR ($5,500–$6,500 range), and find one or two value plays under $5,000 to make the math work. GPP lineups skew more extreme — you might jam $8,200 into a top QB-WR stack and run a $3,500 TE to afford it.

That minimum-salary punt is where beginners bleed points. A $3,000 player who scores 2 fantasy points produces 0.67 points per $1,000. You just burned a roster spot at one-third the efficiency of a league-average play. The savings look attractive on the salary line. They rarely look attractive on the scoreboard.

## Contest Types: Cash Games vs. GPPs and Why Your Strategy Must Change

Cash games and tournaments reward fundamentally different things. Treating them the same is the fastest way to burn through a bankroll.

In a 50/50 or double-up, you need to finish in the top half. That's it. You're not trying to post the highest score in the contest — you're trying to avoid the bottom. Floor matters more than ceiling. A cash lineup leans on high-volume, consistent producers: a $7,400 RB who's touched the ball 20+ times in four straight weeks and has a safe floor around 14 DraftKings points. You don't need fireworks. You need a lineup where every slot returns reasonable points relative to salary, and nobody craters to 3.

GPPs flip the math. In a 5,000-person tournament, you might need to beat 95% of entries to reach a meaningful payout. Consistency gets you nowhere — you need spikes. That $4,600 WR who's 4% owned but draws a shadow-free matchup against a bottom-five secondary? He's a GPP piece. If he catches two touchdowns, you've separated from thousands of lineups that rostered the chalk. If he duds, you lose a $3 entry, not your rent.

| Factor | Cash Games (50/50, Double-Ups) | GPPs (Tournaments) |
|--------|-------------------------------|---------------------|
| Goal | Finish top 50% | Finish top 1–10% |
| Priority | Floor (consistency) | Ceiling (upside) |
| Ownership | Irrelevant — play the best players | Critical — low ownership creates separation |
| Stacking | Optional | Nearly mandatory |
| Risk tolerance | Low — avoid zeros | High — accept duds for spike potential |
| Typical entry | $3–$5 double-ups | $1–$5 single-entry GPPs |
| Bankroll allocation | ~80% of weekly budget | ~20% of weekly budget |

For bankroll management, a standard framework: allocate roughly 80% of your weekly budget to cash games and 20% to GPPs. On a $20 weekly bankroll, that's $16 across a few $3 double-ups and $4 on one or two single-entry tournaments. Single-entry specifically — they prevent you from overexposing yourself to one lineup construction before you've built the reps to diversify intelligently.

## Building Your First Lineup: A Step-by-Step Worked Example

Start with the slate, not with players. Pull up the Vegas totals for Sunday's games and sort by combined over/under. In Week 3 of 2026, say you see Bills-Dolphins at 52.5, Cowboys-Eagles at 49, and Bengals-Ravens at 48.5. Those are your target game environments. A game with a 52.5 total projects roughly 6–7 total touchdowns — that's where the fantasy points live.

Now build on DraftKings. You take Josh Allen at $7,800 and pair him with his top target at $6,600. That's $14,400 on your primary stack. For the bring-back, grab Tyreek Hill at $7,900 — he benefits when Miami is chasing points in a shootout. You're at $22,300 through three roster spots, leaving $27,700 across six remaining slots, or roughly $4,600 average per spot.

Fill the rest with value plays: a $5,200 RB in a plus matchup projected for 14 points, a $4,100 WR who just moved into a starting role after an injury ahead of him. After slotting everyone in, you have $3,800 left for your final spot. Tight, but workable if you've identified a viable option in that salary range during your research.

Here's how the full salary allocation breaks down:

| Slot | Player | Salary | Projected Pts | Pts/$1K |
|------|--------|--------|---------------|---------|
| QB | Josh Allen | $7,800 | 22.5 | 2.88 |
| WR1 | Allen's WR1 (stack) | $6,600 | 16.2 | 2.45 |
| WR2 | Tyreek Hill (bring-back) | $7,900 | 17.8 | 2.25 |
| RB1 | Plus-matchup RB | $5,200 | 14.0 | 2.69 |
| RB2 | Value RB | $4,500 | 11.5 | 2.56 |
| WR3 | New starter (injury ahead) | $4,100 | 10.8 | 2.63 |
| TE | Mid-tier TE | $4,700 | 9.5 | 2.02 |
| FLEX | Volume-based play | $5,400 | 13.0 | 2.41 |
| DST | Game-script DST | $3,800 | 8.0 | 2.11 |
| **Total** | | **$50,000** | **123.3** | **2.47 avg** |

The mistake most people make here — and the single biggest thing worth hammering — is picking players in isolation. You didn't grab Allen because he's good. You grabbed Allen *because* you wanted Bills-Dolphins exposure, then built outward from that decision. Every slot connects back to the game environment.

Three other traps to dodge. Don't roster Thursday night players in GPPs unless their upside is enormous — you're locking in a score before 90% of the slate plays. Don't chase last week's hero at a salary bumped $500 higher. And stop building lineups from the bottom up. "Finding a cheap guy first" leads to fragmented rosters with no correlation between the pieces.

## Stacking and Correlation: The Concept That Separates Beginners From Intermediate Players

The lineup construction section above introduced stacking through the Allen-Hill example. Here's the underlying concept and why it matters so much.

A stack is two or more players from the same game paired in your lineup because their scoring is linked. The logic: when Patrick Mahomes throws a touchdown, someone catches it. If you roster Mahomes but not the pass-catcher, you captured half the play. Roster both, and you captured both sides — passing yards, passing TD, receiving yards, and receiving TD all hit your lineup at once.

That's correlation. It's the single biggest structural edge most beginners miss.

**Stack types worth knowing:**

- **QB + WR1 (primary stack):** The foundation. A QB paired with his top target — Mahomes + Rashee Rice, for example.
- **QB + WR1 + opposing pass-catcher (bring-back):** You're betting the game script produces points on both sides. Stack Mahomes + Rice, then add Garrett Wilson from the opposing team. A shootout benefits your entire lineup.
- **QB + WR + TE (same-team heavy):** More concentrated, more volatile. You need that offense to explode, but when it does, you leapfrog thousands of entries.

> **Tip:** A quick way to evaluate stack strength: multiply the game's over/under by 0.60 to estimate the favorite's implied team total. If a team's implied total is 28+, their passing game becomes a premium stacking environment. Below 21, look elsewhere.

**When stacking matters — and when it doesn't.** In cash games, stacks are optional. You're targeting a safe floor, and correlation adds variance you don't need. GPPs are different. Data from 2026 DraftKings Millionaire contests shows that roughly 85% of first-place finishers used at least a QB-WR pair from the same team. Tournament lineups without a stack are fighting at a structural disadvantage before the games even kick off.

## Where to Find the Numbers: Free Tools and Data Sources for NFL DFS Beginners

You need three data inputs before building any lineup: projections, ownership estimates, and Vegas lines. Everything else is noise until you know how to use these three.

**Projections.** FantasyPros aggregates projections from dozens of sources into a consensus number — free, no subscription required. RotoGrinders and DailyRotoHelp also publish free weekly projections. A projection of "14.2 points" doesn't mean the player will score 14.2. It's a central estimate. The real outcome might fall anywhere from 6 to 24. Think of it as the midpoint of a range, not a promise.

**Ownership estimates.** RotoGrinders publishes free projected ownership percentages before lock. If a $5,200 running back is projected at 28% ownership in a GPP, that number shapes whether you want exposure or a pivot. You won't find this data on the platform itself.

**Vegas lines.** Covers.com and VegasInsider post game totals and spreads for free. A game with a 49.5 over/under signals more scoring opportunity than one sitting at 38. This is the single fastest way to identify stacking environments — and it's where the lineup construction process from earlier sections begins.

**Build a simple spreadsheet.** Pull player salaries from your platform, paste in consensus projections, then add a column: projected points ÷ salary × 1,000. A receiver projected for 13.8 points at $5,400 returns 2.56 points per $1,000. A receiver projected for 15.1 at $7,100 returns 2.13. That one calculation gives you a sortable value ranking across your entire player pool — and it takes ten minutes. Plenty of guides tell you to "find value." This is how you actually measure it before you ever open a lineup optimizer.

```
Sample Google Sheets Setup (Columns A–F):

A: Player Name
B: Position
C: Salary
D: Projected Points (from FantasyPros consensus)
E: Value Score       =D2/(C2/1000)
F: Projected Own%    (from RotoGrinders)

Sort by Column E descending to see your best values.
Filter by Column F to flag high-ownership plays you may
want to fade in GPPs.
```

## Conclusion

Here's the process distilled into a checklist you can run every Sunday morning before lock:

1. **Check the Vegas environment.** Pull game totals and spreads. Flag every game with a combined over/under above 48 and a spread under 7 — those are your primary stacking targets.
2. **Identify your stacks.** Pick a QB-WR pair from one of those games. Add a bring-back from the opposing team to capture both sides of a shootout.
3. **Build around correlation.** Don't assemble nine random "best plays." Your lineup should tell a story — if you're rostering Jalen Hurts, pair him with a Philadelphia pass-catcher, not just whoever has the lowest salary.
4. **Fill value spots.** Run your points-per-$1,000 calculation on the remaining player pool. That $4,800 RB at 2.5 projected points per $1,000 beats the $7,200 name you recognize at 2.2.
5. **Match strategy to contest type.** Cash game? Prioritize floor. GPP? Prioritize ceiling and low ownership.

Your first 50 lineups will be rough. That's expected. But running this process each week keeps you from bleeding money on the mistakes that knock most beginners out before they develop real skill.

## FAQ

### How much money do I need to start playing NFL DFS?

Most platforms let you enter contests for $0.25, so the technical barrier is almost nothing. A more useful number: $20–$50 per week gives you enough entries to actually learn. At that bankroll, you're playing $1–$3 contests — enough to feel the decisions matter, not enough to damage your finances while you figure out roster construction. If you deposit $50, that's roughly 15–20 entries across a Sunday slate. Plenty of reps to build a feel for the process.

### What's the difference between DraftKings and FanDuel for NFL DFS?

The differences sound small but compound fast. DraftKings runs a $50,000 salary cap with full PPR and 6-point passing TDs across a roster that includes QB, RB, RB, WR, WR, WR, TE, FLEX, and DST. FanDuel uses a $60,000 cap with half PPR and 4-point passing TDs across nine slots including a dedicated kicker spot instead of a third WR. That 2-point-per-TD swing means QBs on DraftKings have significantly higher ceilings — a 3-TD game is worth 6 more points than on FanDuel. Full PPR vs. half PPR also shifts target-heavy receivers and pass-catching backs up the value board on DraftKings. Both platforms remain the dominant options in 2026, and many serious players build lineups on both.

### Should beginners play cash games or tournaments first?

Cash games. In a 50/50, you need to finish in the top half — roughly 50th percentile. In a GPP, you often need top 10–20% just to cash at all, and top 1% to hit meaningful prizes. Cash games reward a straightforward process: play high-floor players, avoid unnecessary risk, build a consistent lineup. That's the exact skill set a new player needs to develop first. Once you're regularly cashing in double-ups — hitting the cash line 55–60% of the time across 30+ entries — you have the foundation to start allocating 20% of your bankroll to tournaments, where ownership leverage and ceiling matter more.
