## Core NFL DFS Terminology: The Foundations

Daily fantasy sports (DFS) is simple at the surface: pick players who fit under a salary cap, score the most points, win money. The details are where it gets real.

**Salary cap** is the spending limit for your roster. On DraftKings, you get $50,000 across 9 roster slots (QB, RB, RB, WR, WR, WR, TE, FLEX, DST). FanDuel gives you $60,000 across the same 9-slot structure (QB, RB, RB, WR, WR, WR, TE, FLEX, DEF). Neither platform uses a kicker in classic NFL contests. The higher FanDuel cap number doesn't mean more flexibility — average salaries scale proportionally.

| | DraftKings | FanDuel |
|---|---|---|
| **Salary Cap** | $50,000 | $60,000 |
| **Roster Slots** | 9 (QB, RB, RB, WR, WR, WR, TE, FLEX, DST) | 9 (QB, RB, RB, WR, WR, WR, TE, FLEX, DEF) |
| **PPR Format** | Full PPR (1.0 per reception) | Half PPR (0.5 per reception) |
| **Passing TD Points** | 6 | 4 |
| **Rushing/Receiving TD** | 6 | 6 |
| **300-Yard Passing Bonus** | 3 | None |

**Scoring formats** matter enormously. DraftKings uses full PPR (1 point per reception). FanDuel uses half-PPR (0.5 per reception). That single difference shifts player values — a slot receiver who catches 8 passes for 60 yards scores 14.0 on DraftKings (6.0 receiving yards + 8.0 receptions) but 10.0 on FanDuel (6.0 + 4.0). For a deeper walkthrough of how scoring systems work across platforms, see our [guide to NFL DFS mechanics, scoring, and real examples](https://www.dfsengineer.com/blog/how-nfl-dfs-works-mechanics-scoring-real-examples).

Every contest charges an **entry fee** (the buy-in). The platform keeps a cut called the **rake**, typically 10–15%. A $20 entry on a $20,000 GPP means roughly $2–$3 of your buy-in never reaches the prize pool. Your expected value starts negative by that percentage, so your edge must overcome the rake before you profit.

Here's a quick scored example: say your DraftKings QB throws for 280 yards and 3 TDs. That's 11.2 passing-yard points (280 × 0.04) plus 18.0 TD points (3 × 6) = 29.2 points from one slot. Your full roster might total 160 points across all nine positions — understanding how each slot contributes is the first piece of NFL DFS terminology that actually drives decisions.

```
Worked Example: Scoring a DraftKings QB

Passing yards:   280 × 0.04        = 11.2 pts
Passing TDs:       3 × 6           = 18.0 pts
Completions:      22 × 0 (no pts)  =  0.0 pts
Interceptions:     1 × (-1)        = -1.0 pts
                                    --------
Total:                               28.2 pts

Now add a 40-yard rushing TD:
Rushing yards:    40 × 0.1          =  4.0 pts
Rushing TD:        1 × 6            =  6.0 pts
                                    --------
Grand Total:                         38.2 pts
```

## Contest Type Terminology Every NFL DFS Player Must Know

The contest you enter matters more than the lineup you build. That's not an exaggeration — the same roster can be +EV in one format and -EV in another.

**GPP (Guaranteed Prize Pool):** A tournament where the prize pool is fixed regardless of entries. A $5 entry, 10,000-person GPP on DraftKings might pay $10,000 to first place and nothing to the bottom 70% of the field. Top-heavy payouts reward high ceilings and differentiated builds.

**Cash games** include **50/50s** (top half wins, bottom half loses) and **double-ups** (top ~45% of the field roughly doubles their entry fee). Say you enter a $20 double-up that pays $36 to winners. The $4 gap between $40 collected per pair and $36 paid represents the platform's rake. These formats reward floor and consistency.

| Contest Type | Payout Structure | What Wins | Roster Strategy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **GPP / Tournament** | Top-heavy (top 20–30% paid, big prizes at top) | Ceiling, differentiation | Stacks, leverage, boom-or-bust |
| **50/50** | Top 50% doubles, bottom 50% loses | Floor, consistency | Safe high-floor plays |
| **Double-Up** | Top ~45% roughly doubles entry | Floor, consistency | Safe high-floor plays |
| **Single-Entry GPP** | Top-heavy, one lineup per person | Sharp single reads | Best outright build |
| **Satellite** | Seats into larger contests | Finishing above a cutline | Hybrid of cash/GPP |
| **Showdown/Captain** | Single-game, Captain at 1.5× | Game-specific reads | Captain leverage is key |

**Single-entry** contests limit you to one lineup. **Multi-entry** or **max-entry** contests let you submit many — a "150-max" GPP allows 150 lineups from one account. Mass multi-entry players use portfolio theory; single-entry fields tend to be softer and reward sharper single-build reads.

**Satellites** award seats into bigger contests instead of cash. **Showdown/Captain mode** uses a single game with a Captain slot scoring at 1.5×.

**Overlay** occurs when a GPP doesn't fill — if a $100K guaranteed contest collects only $92K in entry fees, that $8K overlay is free equity for the field.

**Late swap** lets you change players after the main slate locks, as long as their individual game hasn't kicked off. Knowing your terminology around game-time decisions — inactive reports, weather shifts — becomes directly profitable here.

Why does contest type reshape roster construction? In a 50/50 you want the safest 150-point floor possible. In a 10,000-entry GPP, 150 points probably finishes outside the money. You need 180+, which means accepting more volatile players and tighter correlations. Same player pool, completely different logic.

> **Tip:** If you're new to DFS, start with 50/50s and double-ups. The flatter payout structure lets you learn roster construction without needing perfect ownership reads and leverage plays on day one. Once you're consistently cashing in cash games, move a portion of your bankroll into GPPs.

## Ownership and Leverage: The NFL DFS Terms That Separate Winners from Losers

**Chalk** is any player with high expected ownership. **Ownership percentage** is the share of lineups in a contest that roster a given player. **Fading** means leaving that player out. **Leverage** is the edge you gain when a high-owned player underperforms and your lineup doesn't carry the damage. For more on these terms and others, our [NFL DFS glossary](https://www.dfsengineer.com/blog/nfl-dfs-glossary) has quick-reference definitions.

Here's how this works. Say it's a Week 6 GPP with 10,000 entries, and Jalen Hurts is 45% owned. If Hurts scores 28 fantasy points, he helps nearly half the field equally — no separation. But if Hurts throws two picks and finishes at 8 points, 4,500 lineups just cratered. Your lineup, which faded Hurts for a 9%-owned Jayden Daniels who scores 24 points, now sits above almost half the field before you even look at the rest of your roster. That's leverage.

```
Worked Example: Leverage Math in a 10,000-Entry GPP

Scenario: Hurts (45% owned) busts at 8 pts.
          Daniels (9% owned) scores 24 pts.

Your advantage at the QB slot alone:
  24 − 8 = 16 points over 4,500 lineups (45% of field)

Meanwhile, only ~900 other lineups (9%) share your Daniels upside.
You've separated from 45% of the field AND overlap with only 9%.

That's leverage: not just picking the right player,
but picking a right player that most of the field doesn't have.
```

**Projected ownership** is your pre-lock estimate; **actual ownership** is what the contest reveals after lock. The gap between these two numbers matters because mispriced ownership creates value. If you project a receiver at 25% but he comes in at 12%, you missed a spot where being contrarian cost you nothing.

Now consider two lineups projecting 175 points each. Lineup A averages 22% ownership per player. Lineup B averages 8%. In a GPP, Lineup B has meaningfully higher equity because when it hits, fewer lineups overlap with it. Identical ceilings, different payout probability. This distinction — between raw points and *differentiated* points — is the concept most beginners learn last but the one that matters most in tournaments.

## Projection and Ceiling/Floor Terminology in NFL DFS

A player's projection is not a prediction. It's the center of a distribution — the single most likely outcome in a range of many possible outcomes. When you see "Ja'Marr Chase projected 18.2 DraftKings points," that number sits at the midpoint of a curve that might stretch from 4 points (two catches, 30 yards, no TDs) to 42 points (eight catches, 160 yards, two TDs, a rushing score). The full range matters more than the point estimate.

**Floor** is the low end of likely outcomes — roughly the 10th percentile. **Ceiling** is the high end — the 90th percentile. **Median** falls in between and often sits slightly below the mean projection because big games skew the average upward. A running back with a 14-point projection might have a floor of 6 and a ceiling of 28. A boom-bust deep threat with the same 14-point projection might carry a floor of 2 and a ceiling of 38. Same projection, completely different profiles.

```
Worked Example: Two Players, Same Projection, Different Profiles

Player A — Workhorse RB ($7,000)
  Floor (10th %ile):   6 pts
  Median:             13 pts
  Projection (mean):  14 pts
  Ceiling (90th %ile): 28 pts
  → Tight range. Good for cash games.

Player B — Deep-Threat WR ($7,000)
  Floor (10th %ile):   2 pts
  Median:             10 pts
  Projection (mean):  14 pts
  Ceiling (90th %ile): 38 pts
  → Wide range. GPP weapon if the game script hits.

Same $7,000 salary. Same 14-pt projection.
In a 50/50, you want Player A.
In a 10,000-entry GPP, Player B gives you more equity.
```

**Correlation** is what makes stacking work. When Patrick Mahomes throws a 45-yard touchdown, Travis Kelce or Rashee Rice is catching it. A QB-WR stack doesn't just add two projections together — it links their upside. On a slate where Kansas City's game carries a 51.5-point total, stacking Mahomes with Rice and bringing back the opposing team's top receiver creates a **game stack** that captures scoring from both sides of a high-total contest. Those three players' ceilings aren't independent; they move together when the game script cooperates.

A **smash spot** describes a player whose projection, matchup, and game environment all align toward ceiling outcomes. The term maps to specific inputs. A **pace-up** game is one where the Vegas total sits well above the slate median — say 49.5 in a slate averaging 43.5 — signaling more plays, more points, more DFS-relevant volume. When a receiver faces a bottom-five pass defense in a pace-up spot with a spread tight enough to keep both teams throwing, that's a smash spot backed by real numbers, not gut feel.

A **fragile projection** flags a player whose number depends on a narrow path. Say a running back is projected for 15 DraftKings points, and 5.4 of those points come from a touchdown expectation of 0.9 (0.9 × 6 = 5.4). Strip that TD away and you're looking at roughly 9.6 points. Understanding this helps you decide where that player belongs: probably fine for cash games where you need his median, risky for GPPs where his ceiling isn't differentiated enough to justify the fragility.

> **Warning:** A projection that leans heavily on touchdown expectation is inherently fragile. Touchdowns are high-variance events — a runner can get 18 carries inside the 10-yard line across a season and score anywhere from 4 to 14 TDs. Before you trust a projection, check how much of it comes from TD equity. If more than a third of the projected points come from expected TDs, treat the floor as materially lower than the projection implies.

Vegas lines aren't decoration. They're the most efficient public measure of game environment. A total of 47 means sportsbooks expect roughly 47 combined points scored. A spread of -7 implies the favored team controls pace and the trailing team chases through the air. These numbers feed directly into pass rate estimates, red zone opportunity projections, and ultimately your player ceilings. Ignore them and you're building lineups without the single best contextual anchor available.

## Bankroll and Process Terminology for NFL DFS

Your **bankroll** is the total amount of money you've set aside exclusively for DFS. Not your checking account. Not money you might need. A separated, dedicated amount. A **unit** is the standard fraction of that bankroll you risk on a single entry. Most serious players define one unit as 0.5–1% of their bankroll.

Here's how this works. You start the NFL season with a $2,000 bankroll. At 1% per unit, each unit is $20. On a 13-game Sunday main slate, you enter three $20 cash games and two $20 GPPs — five units, or $100, or 5% of your bankroll on a single slate. Conservative and sustainable.

```
Worked Example: Weekly Bankroll Allocation

Starting bankroll: $2,000
Unit size (1%):    $20

Sunday Main Slate plan:
  3 × $20 cash games (50/50s)    = $60
  2 × $20 GPPs (single-entry)    = $40
                                  -----
  Total risk this slate:           $100  (5% of bankroll)

If bankroll drops to $1,500 mid-season:
  New unit size (1%):              $15
  You scale entries down accordingly.
  This is how you survive variance.
```

**ROI** (return on investment) measures profit divided by total entry fees. A player who spends $10,000 in entries across a season and profits $800 has an 8% ROI ($800 ÷ $10,000). That's genuinely strong. Most winning players land between 5–15%.

**+EV** means positive expected value — your entries, on average, return more than they cost. But variance makes short samples meaningless. A +EV player with 7% ROI can easily lose money across 50 contests. Stretch that to 500 entries and the edge almost always surfaces. This is why **volume** matters: not grinding for the sake of it, but letting your edge compound past the noise.

**Mass multi-entry (MME)** describes entering a GPP with many lineups — sometimes up to a 150-max cap. **Portfolio construction** is the discipline behind it. Twenty lineups in the same GPP shouldn't be twenty random builds. Some should share a core stack; others should diverge into different game environments. You're balancing **correlation** within your portfolio (lineups that win together in the right game script) against **diversification** (coverage across multiple potential slate outcomes). Building 20 lineups that all need the same QB to smash defeats the purpose entirely.

This slice of NFL DFS terminology turns bankroll management from a vague concept into a system with rules you can follow week to week. ## Advanced NFL DFS Terminology: Sim, Optimizer, and Correlation Concepts

An **optimizer** takes your projections, salary constraints, and roster rules, then solves for the highest-projected lineup (or set of lineups) mathematically. It's a constraint-satisfaction engine — nothing more. Feed it bad projections and it spits out a bad lineup, fast. The output is only as good as the inputs.

A **simulator (sim)** goes further. Instead of finding one optimal lineup, it runs thousands of contest simulations — often 10,000+ — randomizing player outcomes across their distributions. A sim might tell you that a $7,200 receiver with a 14.5-point projection finishes as a top-three WR in 18% of simulations, which matters more for GPPs than the projection alone.

| Tool | What It Does | Best For | Key Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Optimizer** | Finds highest-projected lineup(s) given salary + roster constraints | Generating a starting lineup or cash-game build | Only uses point projections — ignores variance and ownership |
| **Simulator** | Runs thousands of randomized contest outcomes across player distributions | GPP lineup selection, setting exposures | Garbage in, garbage out — distributions must be realistic |

**Exposure** is the percentage of your lineup portfolio that includes a given player. If you build 20 lineups and Ja'Marr Chase appears in 12 of them, that's 60% exposure. **Uniqueness** measures how different each lineup is from the rest of your set — low uniqueness means you're basically running the same lineup with minor swaps, which wastes multi-entry slots.

A **correlation matrix** quantifies how players' outcomes move together. QB-WR1 stacks on the same team typically carry correlations of +0.3 to +0.5. **Secondary correlation** captures less obvious relationships: a running back on the opposing team correlates positively with a game script where his team leads, which itself connects to the opposing QB throwing more in catch-up mode. **Negative correlation** is real and useful — a team's rushing attack and the opposing defense's pass rush are inversely related to the passing game ceiling of that same team.

**Lock** is when a player's game kicks off and their roster slot becomes uneditable. The **swap window** is the time between your initial lineup submission and lock. Here's where **confirmation bias** quietly costs people points: you build a lineup Saturday night featuring a questionable WR, and by Sunday morning you've convinced yourself he'll play because you want the lineup to hold. Checking the inactive report mechanically — treating every questionable tag as a genuine decision point rather than a box to check — is a discipline problem more than a knowledge problem.

> **Tip:** Set a hard rule for late swap: if a player you rostered is listed as questionable on Sunday morning, identify a specific replacement *before* you check for positive reports. This forces you to evaluate the swap on its merits rather than anchoring to your original lineup. The best late-swap decisions are made before emotions get involved.

Game-environment terms round out the advanced terminology you'll encounter. **Pace** refers to plays per game; a team running 68 plays versus 58 creates roughly 17% more opportunity volume ((68 − 58) ÷ 58 ≈ 0.17). **Pass rate** isolates how many of those plays are dropbacks. **Red zone opportunities** measure trips inside the 20. These aren't abstract stats — they're the raw inputs that feed projections and define ceilings. A team implied for 28 points with a 63% pass rate and top-five pace creates a fundamentally different projection environment than one implied for 28 points with a run-heavy scheme and bottom-ten pace.

## How NFL DFS Terminology Maps to Your Weekly Process

Every term defined above corresponds to a specific moment in your week. Here's how they chain together when you sit down to build lineups for a Week 8 main slate. If you want a structured walkthrough of this entire process from start to finish, our [beginner's guide to building your first lineup](https://www.dfsengineer.com/blog/nfl-dfs-beginners-guide-build-your-first-lineup-2026) covers each step.

**Start with game environment.** You pull Vegas totals and spreads. Kansas City–Miami shows a 51.5 total and a 3-point spread. That's a pace-up spot for both offenses — high total, tight game, projected pass rates above 60%. You flag it as a ceiling environment.

**Set projections.** Your model spits out a median of 19.2 DraftKings points for Tua Tagovailoa with a ceiling north of 28. That range of outcomes matters more than the single number. You note his floor sits around 10 — fragile if Miami falls behind script, but the game environment says otherwise.

**Choose contest type.** You're entering a $5 GPP with 10,000 entries. That field size demands differentiation, not safety.

**Estimate ownership and find leverage.** Tagovailoa projects at 18% ownership. Patrick Mahomes projects at 24%. You build a Tagovailoa–Tyreek Hill stack at combined ~30% ownership instead of chasing the chalk Mahomes stack. Similar ceiling, lower combined ownership, better GPP equity.

**Set exposures.** Across your 20 MME lineups, you cap Tagovailoa at 35% exposure and Mahomes at 15%. Diversification, not conviction.

```
Weekly Process Checklist (mapped to terminology):

1. Game Environment  → Vegas totals, spreads, pace, pass rate
2. Projections       → Floor, ceiling, median, fragile projections
3. Contest Selection → GPP vs. cash, field size, payout structure
4. Ownership         → Chalk, projected ownership, leverage, fading
5. Correlation       → Stacks, game stacks, bring-backs
6. Build             → Optimizer/sim, salary cap, roster construction
7. Exposure          → Portfolio construction, uniqueness, diversification
8. Late Swap         → Lock times, inactive reports, confirmation bias check
```

That sequence — environment, projection, contest type, ownership, exposure — is your NFL DFS terminology in action. Each term is a decision, not a definition.

## Conclusion

Knowing that a GPP is a guaranteed prize pool doesn't make you profitable. Knowing that a 45% chalk QB who busts reshapes the entire leaderboard — and building your process around that knowledge — does. That's the difference between memorizing NFL DFS terminology and actually using it.

Every term in this reference maps to a specific decision you make each week. "Ceiling" isn't a vocabulary word; it's the reason you roster a boom-or-bust WR3 in a 10,000-entry GPP instead of a safe RB2. "Exposure" isn't abstract portfolio theory; it's the concrete choice to cap Ja'Marr Chase at 40% of your 20-lineup MME build because his 28% projected ownership doesn't offer enough leverage at full exposure. "Rake" isn't a footnote; it's the cut that means in a double-up paying $36 on a $20 entry — where roughly 45% of the field cashes — you need to finish in the top 45% consistently, a bar meaningfully higher than a coin flip once the platform's cut is factored in.

A structured process is built from these terms, connected in sequence. You don't skip from projections to lineup locks. You move through each decision point — totals, correlations, ownership estimates, exposure caps — and each one uses the terminology above as a working tool, not a definition to memorize.
