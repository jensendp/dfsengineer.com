"Good matchup" gets thrown around constantly in DFS content, usually
pointing at one stat — points allowed to the position — without the
context that makes that stat useful. This page explains what actually
goes into a real matchup read; each week's post below applies it to that
week's real slate.

## What "points allowed" actually measures

A defense's fantasy points allowed to a position, relative to league
average, is a real signal — it's a multiplier on that position's baseline
projection (my own model's `matchup` factor). But it's a *cumulative*,
backward-looking number, which means it can mislead in a few specific
ways:

- **Small samples early in the season — and zero samples in Week 1
  specifically.** Three games of data can be driven by one outlier
  performance, not a real defensive weakness. Week 1 is a sharper
  version of the same problem: there's no current-season data to draw
  on at all, so the multiplier falls back entirely to how that defense
  performed in prior seasons. Real data, not invented — but it's a
  roster and scheme from a year (or more) ago, not this one.
- **Injuries on the defense.** A defense that's allowed a lot to receivers
  because their top cornerback has been hurt for six weeks isn't
  necessarily still a soft matchup once that player is back.
- **It doesn't know about game script.** A defense can be tough against
  the run in points-allowed terms simply because teams rarely need to run
  against them — they're usually trailing. That's a different signal than
  "hard to run against."

## The other half: game environment

A matchup read isn't complete without the game-level context:

- **Vegas total and spread.** A high implied team total means more
  expected scoring plays for that offense, independent of who they're
  playing. A big spread tells you which team is more likely to be
  throwing from behind (favors that team's pass-catchers, and the other
  team's positive-script runner).
- **Pace.** More plays run means more opportunities for everyone on the
  field, on both sides of the ball.

A genuinely good matchup combines a favorable points-allowed multiplier
*with* a game environment that supports it — a soft matchup in a game
projected for 35 total points matters more than the same matchup in a
game projected for 38 with one team a 17-point favorite and running clock
by the third quarter.
